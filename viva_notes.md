# Viva / Interview Notes: AGV Fleet-Sizing Simulation

Every number below comes from `results/summary.txt`, `results/fleet_sizing.csv` and `results/paired_comparison.csv`, generated by `python main.py` with base seed 42 and 20 replications. If you change anything and rerun, re-read those files before quoting numbers.

## 1. The 60-second explanation

> "I built a discrete-event simulation in Python and SimPy of a four-station production line: raw store, two machines and a finished-goods area on a 300 m straight route. AGVs carry parts between stations. Parts arrive randomly, every 4 minutes on average, and machining times are fixed at 7 and 9 minutes. I tested 1 to 5 AGVs under two dispatch rules, first-in-first-out and nearest-pickup. Each case ran 20 times with controlled random seeds, and I report means with 95% confidence intervals.
>
> Machine 2 is the bottleneck at 6.67 parts per hour, so throughput can never exceed that. FIFO needed 3 AGVs to reach 95% of the best throughput. Nearest-pickup needed only 2, because it naturally prioritises downstream moves. The price is that raw-material moves are starved: with 2 AGVs they wait about 99 minutes on average, against 70 under FIFO. Nearest-pickup also cut empty travel by 8–11% at every fleet size. All inputs are illustrative assumptions, so this is a methodology demonstration, not a claim about a real plant."

## 3. Equations

```
Travel time      t = d / (v × 60)            [min], d in m, v in m/s  (v×60 = 48 m/min)
Job duration     t_a → t_a + d_empty/48 + 0.25 + d_loaded/48 + 0.25
Throughput       TH = completions at FG in [60, 540) / 8 h
Waiting time     W̄ = mean(t_assigned − t_created) over jobs created in the window and assigned
Empty distance   D = Σ 48 × overlap(empty-drive interval, window) / 1000   [km]
Utilisation      U = Σ overlap([t_assigned, t_dropoff_end], window) / (N × 480)
95% CI           x̄ ± t(0.975, 19) × s/√20 = x̄ ± 2.093 × s/√20
Fleet sizing     N* = min{ N : TH̄(N) ≥ 0.95 × max_N TH̄(N) }
Bottleneck       capacity_i = 60 / p_i;  bottleneck = station with the smallest capacity
```

## 4. Assumptions and limitations (say them before you are asked)

- The inputs are **illustrative**, not plant data.
- The arrival rate (15 /h) is far above the bottleneck capacity (6.67 /h), so queues grow all shift and there is **no steady state**.
- One straight path. No traffic, collisions, blocking, acceleration, batteries or breakdowns.
- One part per AGV. Unlimited buffers. Fixed machine times.
- AGVs park where they drop off. They all start at the raw store.
- Greedy dispatch rules only. No repositioning and no anti-starvation rule.
- Waiting-time averages for 1–2 AGVs are biased low, because jobs never assigned by minute 540 are censored.

## 5. Likely questions and answers

**Q1. What is discrete-event simulation, and why not a spreadsheet?**
A DES advances the clock from one event to the next (arrival, machine finish, AGV arrival) instead of in fixed time steps. The state only changes at events. A spreadsheet can give average capacities, but it cannot capture queueing interactions. Examples: an AGV being busy elsewhere when a part finishes, or randomness in arrivals creating temporary congestion. DES models those interactions directly.

**Q2. Why SimPy? How are processes, resources and events used in your code?**
SimPy is a free, well-documented Python DES library. Its concepts map onto the factory:

- Each part is a **process** (`component()`, a generator) that `yield`s while it waits.
- Machining and driving are **timeouts**.
- Each machine is a **Resource with capacity 1**; its built-in queue is the unlimited buffer.
- Each transport job has a `done` **event**. The part waits on it, and the AGV process triggers it after drop-off.

I didn't model AGVs as a SimPy Resource. A Resource serves requests strictly first-come-first-served, and nearest-pickup needs to compare all idle AGVs with all waiting jobs. So a small `dispatch()` function does the pairing. It is called whenever a job is created or an AGV becomes free.

**Q3. Explain the two dispatch rules precisely.**
FIFO: take the oldest waiting job and give it to the AGV that has been idle longest. Nearest-pickup: among all (idle AGV, waiting job) pairs, pick the one with the smallest empty distance `|x_AGV − x_pickup|`. Ties are broken by older job, then lower AGV ID. Both rules use identical travel equations, the same model and the same metrics. Only the selection function differs.

**Q4. Which rule is better?**
It depends on the metric, and I let the data decide.

- **Throughput:** nearest-pickup is significantly higher with 1–2 AGVs. There is no significant difference with 3–5.
- **Empty travel:** nearest-pickup is significantly lower at every fleet size.
- **Average transport wait:** nearest-pickup is lower with 1–3 AGVs and no different with 4. It is very slightly higher with 5, by 0.019 min (about 1 second): significant but practically irrelevant.
- **Fairness:** nearest-pickup starves raw-material moves (RM→M1 waits 263 min vs 176 min with 1 AGV; 99 vs 70 min with 2 AGVs). So "better" depends on what the factory values.

**Q5. Why does nearest-pickup give so much more throughput with 1–2 AGVs?**
After dropping at M1 or M2, the nearest job is usually at the same station (0 m away). That job is the next downstream move, so the AGV keeps feeding M2 and clearing finished parts. FIFO serves jobs in creation order. Because parts arrive at 15 /h, most waiting jobs are RM→M1 moves, so FIFO spends scarce AGV time pushing raw parts into M1's queue instead of moving parts that will become finished goods.

**Q6. What is the bottleneck, and how do you know?**
Machine 2. Its capacity is 60/9 = 6.67 /h, against 8.57 /h for M1 and 15 /h of arrivals. The simulation agrees:

- M2 utilisation is 100% whenever there are enough AGVs;
- throughput plateaus at about 6.68 /h;
- a queue of about 16 parts builds in front of M2.

With 1–2 AGVs under FIFO, the AGV fleet is the bottleneck instead: AGV utilisation is 100% and M2 is starved (50% busy with 1 AGV).

**Q7. Why can measured throughput (6.68 /h) be higher than 6.67 /h?**
The 480-minute window can contain ⌊480/9⌋ + 1 = 54 completions, which is 6.75 /h, because M2 may already be part-way through a job at minute 60. The code checks that no replication ever exceeds this physical limit.

**Q8. How did you decide the fleet size?**
For each policy, I took the highest mean throughput among the tested sizes and the 95% threshold of it. Then I chose the smallest fleet whose mean throughput reaches that threshold: FIFO → 3 AGVs, nearest → 2 AGVs. This is relative to the tested sizes 1–5, not the true capacity of a real plant. The code also checks for a plateau: throughput changed by only 0.1% and 0.0% from 4 to 5 AGVs, so the plateau is established. If throughput were still rising strongly at 5, the code would warn that the tested range is too small.

**Q9. What are the random seeds for, and what are common random numbers?**
Replication r uses seed 42 + r for the arrival stream, the only random input. So results are exactly reproducible, which the tests verify. Because the seed depends only on r, every policy and fleet size sees *exactly the same* arrivals in replication r; the code checks this too. This is called **common random numbers**. It means differences between policies come from the policy, not from luck in the arrivals. It also allows a paired comparison with much narrower confidence intervals.

**Q10. How are the confidence intervals calculated, and what do they mean?**
For each metric, I take the 20 replication results and compute the mean x̄ and the sample standard deviation s (dividing by n − 1). The interval is x̄ ± 2.093 × s/√20, where 2.093 is the t value for 95% with 19 degrees of freedom. I use t rather than z because σ is unknown and n is small. Meaning: if the whole 20-replication experiment were repeated many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true long-run mean *of the model*. The interval says nothing about how well the model matches a real factory.

**Q11. Why 20 replications and not one long run?**
A single run gives one random outcome with no measure of variability. Independent replications give independent samples, so a valid standard deviation and CI can be computed. A long run would also be misleading here, because the system is overloaded and queues keep growing.

**Q12. What is the warm-up period, and is 60 minutes enough?**
The model starts empty, with no parts and idle machines, which is unrealistic. The first 60 minutes are discarded so that the empty start doesn't bias the results. With 3 AGVs, M2 starts its first job between minutes 13 and 27 across the 20 replications, so 60 minutes removes the start-up effect on throughput. But because arrivals exceed capacity, queues grow for the whole run. There is no steady state for a warm-up to reach. For a stable factory I would choose the warm-up from a time plot of the output (Welch's method).

**Q13. How do you handle activities that cross the warm-up or end boundary?**
Only the part of each interval that lies inside [60, 540) is counted, using `overlap = max(0, min(end, 540) − max(start, 60))`. This applies to AGV busy time (utilisation) and driving time; distance is speed × overlapping time. A part counts as produced only if its drop-off at FG happens inside the window. Waiting time uses jobs *created* inside the window. Jobs still unassigned at the end are reported separately, because their true wait is unknown.

**Q14. Why does utilisation fall when you add AGVs while throughput stays flat?**
Once M2 is continuously fed, the total transport work per hour is fixed by the flow rates, roughly 150–160 AGV-minutes per hour. Spreading the same work over more vehicles lowers each one's busy fraction: 88% with 3, 66% with 4, 53% with 5 under FIFO. The extra AGVs only shorten transport waits (3.3 → 0.1 min). That is diminishing returns.

**Q15. Why does empty distance *increase* from 1 to 3 AGVs?**
Because more jobs get done. One FIFO AGV moves only 9.6 km loaded in 8 h, while three move 24.7 km, and empty trips grow with the number of jobs. A fairer efficiency measure is the empty-to-loaded ratio, which is also in the CSV. With 3–5 AGVs it is about 1.22–1.24 for FIFO and 1.08–1.14 for nearest-pickup.

**Q16. How did you verify the model is correct (verification)?**
After every run, `check_invariants()` confirms:

- each part's event sequence is exactly RM → M1 → M2 → FG;
- an AGV never starts a job before finishing the previous one, and a run-time check also stops the simulation if an AGV tries to pick up while loaded;
- machines never overlap;
- durations are non-negative;
- observed drop-off times equal planned ones;
- utilisation is within [0, 1].

The pytest suite (23 tests) also covers:

- travel-time units and dispatch tie-breaking, on hand-built cases;
- the CI formula, against a hand calculation;
- the fleet-sizing edge cases;
- that a planted error is actually detected.

I also hand-checked utilisation from the simulated distances: 88.2% by hand vs 88.4% simulated for FIFO with 3 AGVs.

**Q17. Verification vs validation?**
Verification asks: did I build the model right? Does the code do what I intended? That is what the checks and tests above do. Validation asks: did I build the right model? Does it match the real system? I have **not** validated against a real factory, because the inputs are assumptions. To validate, I would collect real arrival logs, cycle times and AGV logs, fit the distributions, run the model with the real fleet and rule, and compare its outputs with plant records using confidence intervals.

**Q18. What are the main limitations, and what would you add next?**

- The system is overloaded with no release control, so WIP grows without bound.
- No traffic, blocking, batteries or breakdowns.
- Unlimited buffers. Deterministic machines.
- Greedy rules with no anti-starvation.

Next steps:

- test an arrival rate below the bottleneck capacity;
- add CONWIP or kanban release control;
- add battery and charging behaviour;
- add a hybrid rule ("nearest unless a job has waited more than X minutes");
- add a sensitivity analysis on speed and machine times.

**Q19. Why exponential interarrival times?**
They describe arrivals that occur independently at a constant average rate (a Poisson process). That is a standard first assumption when no data exist. The exponential distribution is memoryless, which is a simplification. Real arrivals are often more regular, for example from a scheduled release. With real data I would fit and test a distribution.

**Q20. Could nearest-pickup ever be worse than FIFO for throughput?**
In this model, with these inputs, it never was significantly worse. Logically it could be. If arrivals were below the bottleneck capacity and raw parts were starved long enough to leave M1 or M2 idle, throughput would suffer. That is why I don't claim nearest-pickup is generally superior. The result depends on the overloaded arrival assumption.
